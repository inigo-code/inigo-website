Ask a manager how their team is doing and the answer is usually about output: targets met, deadlines kept. Ask how the *work* is treating the team and the answer gets vaguer. Quality of work life is the name for that second question, and it is one of the areas I research. This piece turns the most widely used framework for it into a practical audit.

## Where the idea comes from

The phrase *quality of working life* became common in the late 1960s and 1970s, when researchers and unions in the United States and Europe began to ask whether jobs designed only for efficiency were costing people too much. In 1973 Richard E. Walton of Harvard Business School published an article in the *Sloan Management Review* with a simple title, "Quality of working life: What is it?" His answer was eight criteria. Half a century later, they remain among the most widely used ways of measuring QWL, including in Indian studies of teachers, nurses, bank staff and IT employees.

## The eight questions

Here are Walton's criteria, each followed by the question I would ask a team.

**1. Adequate and fair compensation.** *Is the pay enough to live on, and does it feel fair next to others doing similar work?* Walton separates adequacy from fairness. A good salary that is visibly out of line with a colleague's can do as much harm as a low one.

**2. Safe and healthy working conditions.** *Are the hours reasonable, and is the place physically and mentally safe?* For office and campus work, the risk is less often physical injury than long hours, constant availability and poor ergonomics.

**3. Immediate opportunity to use and develop human capacities.** *Do people have some control over how they work, a variety of skills to use, and whole pieces of work they can call their own?* This is the criterion closest to job design. A job broken into fragments, closely supervised, scores low however well it pays.

**4. Opportunity for continued growth and security.** *Can people see a future here: learning, a path to progress, and reasonable security?* In India this often turns on contract and temporary roles, where the same work is done without the same future.

**5. Social integration in the work organisation.** *Do people feel they belong, free of prejudice, with supportive colleagues?* Walton included freedom from prejudice and a sense of community. Cliques and status barriers undermine it.

**6. Constitutionalism in the work organisation.** *Are there clear rights here: privacy, the freedom to speak up, fair treatment and a fair process when things go wrong?* This is Walton's most unusual criterion, and often the weakest in practice.

**7. Work and the total life space.** *Does the job leave enough time and energy for family and life outside work?* Walton framed it as balance: work should not regularly demand travel, overtime or transfers that crowd out the rest of life.

**8. Social relevance of work life.** *Can people be proud of what the organisation does in society?* Employees who see their employer as socially responsible value their own work more.

## How to run the audit

1. **Ask, don't assume.** A short anonymous questionnaire, with one or two items for each criterion on a five-point scale, gives a first picture. Validated QWL scales built on Walton's criteria are widely used in research and can be adapted.
2. **Look for the widest gaps,** not the average. A team can be well paid and still score very low on rights or balance.
3. **Talk about the results with the team.** The numbers point to where to look; the conversations tell you why.
4. **Change the job, not just the person.** If the gap is in criterion 3 or 7, a wellness workshop will not close it. Fewer reports, clearer priorities, more discretion over how the work is done, and protected time off will.
5. **Repeat in six or twelve months** and see what moved.

## Why this matters for stress

In my research on occupational stress in knowledge-intensive sectors, quality of work life keeps appearing as the other side of the same coin. The demands that cause strain, and the missing resources that leave people unable to cope, map closely onto Walton's criteria. I wrote about that link in [Workplace stress is a design problem, not a personal failing](/articles/stress-is-a-design-problem/). An organisation that takes QWL seriously is, in effect, preventing stress before it starts.

## A note for students

If you are writing a B.B.A. or M.B.A. project on quality of work life, Walton's eight criteria make a clear structure for a questionnaire. Define each criterion in your own words, cite Walton (1973), use a scale that has been validated, and test its reliability on your own sample before you analyse the results.
