I train students in soft skills and speak at colleges, and in September 2026 I was guest of honour at Bishop Heber College's one-day programme on effective public speaking. The question students ask me most is some version of "How do I stop being scared?" My honest answer is that confident speakers are not born that way. They follow a method, and they practise it. Here is the method.

## 1. Decide the one message

Before you write a slide or a sentence, finish this line: *If my audience remembers only one thing, it should be…* That is your message. Every point, story and number in the talk either supports it or comes out.

Most weak presentations fail here. They try to say everything the speaker knows, and the audience leaves remembering nothing.

## 2. Use a structure the audience can follow

Listeners cannot scroll back. Give them a shape:

- **Opening (about 10%).** A question, a surprising fact or a short real example, then your message in one sentence. Never open with "Good morning, today I am going to talk about…" and a long thank-you list; keep courtesies brief.
- **Body (about 80%).** Three points, each supporting the message. Three is easy to remember. Signpost them: "First… Second… Finally…"
- **Close (about 10%).** Repeat the message in fresh words and, if it fits, say what you want the audience to do.

For short answers in interviews, group discussions and vivas, use **PREP**: **P**oint, **R**eason, **E**xample, **P**oint. "I'd choose a franchise model (point), because it lets the company grow without heavy capital (reason). That is how many Indian food brands have expanded (example). So for this firm, franchising is the better route (point)."

## 3. Rehearse out loud, standing up

Reading your notes silently is not rehearsal. Your mouth needs to practise the words. Stand up, speak at full volume, and time yourself.

- **First run:** you will find the parts that do not flow.
- **Second run:** fix them.
- **Third run:** time it, and cut if you are over.

Memorise the opening, the links between points and the closing line. Do not memorise the rest word for word; know your points in order and speak them naturally.

## 4. Use your voice and body on purpose

- **Pace:** most nervous speakers rush. Slow down to a little below conversation speed.
- **Pauses:** pause after an important line. Silence that feels long to you feels natural to the audience.
- **Eye contact:** look at one person for a full sentence, then another, across the room.
- **Stance:** stand still with your weight on both feet. Let your hands move naturally; avoid gripping the lectern.
- **Volume:** speak to the last row. If there is a microphone, keep it at a steady distance.

## 5. Make slides help, not compete

Slides are for the audience, not your script. One idea per slide, a few words, large type, and a picture or chart where it explains better than text. If you are reading your slides aloud, the audience does not need you.

## 6. Handle nerves

Nervousness is your body preparing to perform. It does not go away entirely, even for experienced speakers, but you can manage it:

- **Breathe slowly** before you begin, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath.
- **Know your first two sentences by heart.** The first minute is the hardest; once it is past, most nerves settle.
- **Find friendly faces** and speak to them first.
- **If you lose your place,** pause, look at your notes, and continue. The audience rarely notices.

## 7. Handle questions

Listen to the whole question. Repeat or rephrase it so everyone hears it. Answer briefly, using PREP. If you do not know, say so and offer to find out; that earns more respect than bluffing.

## 8. Speaking in a second language

Many of my students think in Tamil and present in English. That is a strength, not a handicap. Audiences need clarity, not a particular accent. Speak slightly slower, finish your word endings, choose simple words over impressive ones, and pause between ideas. If a word will not come, describe it. Fluency grows with practice, not before it.

## A practice plan for one month

- **Week 1:** record a one-minute talk on your phone about anything you know well. Watch it once and note one thing to improve.
- **Week 2:** answer three interview-style questions aloud using PREP.
- **Week 3:** give a three-minute talk to two friends, and ask them what they remembered.
- **Week 4:** volunteer to speak in class, at a club or at an event.

Confidence comes after the practice, not before it. Start small, and start this week.
