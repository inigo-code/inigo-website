## The core trade-off

Every firm going abroad must decide how much of its own money, people and control to commit. The modes of entry sit on a ladder: the higher you climb, the more **control** and potential **profit** you gain, and the more **investment** and **risk** you take on.

## 1. Exporting

Producing at home and selling abroad.

- **Indirect exporting:** through intermediaries such as export houses or trading companies.
- **Direct exporting:** through the firm's own export department, overseas agents or distributors.

**Advantages:** low investment and risk; a good way to test a market.
**Disadvantages:** transport costs, tariffs and trade barriers; little control over marketing abroad.

## 2. Licensing

The firm (licensor) allows a foreign firm (licensee) to use its intellectual property — patents, trademarks, technology or know-how — in return for a **royalty** or fee.

**Advantages:** fast entry with very little capital; avoids trade barriers.
**Disadvantages:** limited control over quality; the licensee may become a future competitor.

## 3. Franchising

A deeper form of licensing, common in services. The franchisor provides its brand together with a complete **business system** — operating methods, training and support — and the franchisee runs outlets to those standards in return for fees and royalties.

*Example:* McDonald's operates in India through local master franchisees.

**Advantages:** rapid expansion using franchisees' capital and local knowledge.
**Disadvantages:** maintaining quality and brand standards across many outlets.

## 4. Contract manufacturing

The firm contracts a foreign producer to make its products, while it keeps control of design, marketing and sales.

**Advantages:** low investment; access to cheaper production.
**Disadvantages:** less control over the production process and working conditions; risk to reputation.

## 5. Joint venture (JV)

The firm shares ownership and control of a new enterprise with a local partner.

*Example:* Maruti Udyog was set up in the early 1980s as a joint venture between the Government of India and Suzuki Motor Corporation of Japan; it is now Maruti Suzuki India.

**Advantages:** the partner's local knowledge, contacts and distribution; shared risk; may be required by host-country rules in some sectors.
**Disadvantages:** conflicts over control, strategy and profit-sharing; partners may part ways.

## 6. Wholly owned subsidiary

The firm owns 100 per cent of its foreign operation, either by building it from scratch (**greenfield investment**) or by buying an existing company (**acquisition**).

*Example:* Tata Motors acquired the British carmaker Jaguar Land Rover in 2008.

**Advantages:** full control over operations, technology and profits.
**Disadvantages:** highest investment and risk; the firm bears all losses and must manage an unfamiliar environment alone.

## Comparison at a glance

| Mode | Investment | Control | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exporting | Low | Low | Low |
| Licensing | Very low | Low | Low |
| Franchising | Low | Moderate | Low |
| Contract manufacturing | Low | Moderate | Moderate |
| Joint venture | Moderate to high | Shared | Shared |
| Wholly owned subsidiary | High | Full | High |

## Factors that shape the choice

- **Firm factors:** resources, international experience, how valuable and easy to copy its technology is.
- **Market factors:** market size and growth, competition, cultural distance.
- **Environmental factors:** political stability, trade barriers, government rules on foreign ownership, exchange-rate risk.
