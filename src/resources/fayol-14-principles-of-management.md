## Who Fayol was

**Henri Fayol** (1841–1925) was a French mining engineer who rose to be managing director of the mining and metallurgical company Commentry-Fourchambault-Decazeville, and turned it around from near-bankruptcy. In 1916 he published *Administration industrielle et générale*, translated into English in 1949 as *General and Industrial Management*. Where F. W. Taylor studied the worker's task, Fayol studied the manager's job. His approach is called **administrative management**.

## The five functions of management

1. **Planning** (*prévoyance*): looking ahead and drawing up a plan of action.
2. **Organising:** building the structure of people and materials to carry out the plan.
3. **Commanding:** directing people so that the organisation runs.
4. **Coordinating:** binding together and harmonising all activities and efforts.
5. **Controlling:** checking that everything happens according to the plan and the orders given.

Modern textbooks usually list planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling, but the structure comes from Fayol.

## The 14 principles

| # | Principle | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Division of work** | Specialisation raises efficiency and skill. | In a bank, separate staff handle loans, deposits and foreign exchange. |
| 2 | **Authority and responsibility** | Authority to give orders must go with responsibility for results, in balance. | A project lead who answers for a deadline must have the authority to assign the work. |
| 3 | **Discipline** | Obedience, respect for agreements and fair penalties, which need good superiors at every level. | Clear attendance rules applied the same way to everyone. |
| 4 | **Unity of command** | Each employee takes orders from one superior only. | A sales executive reporting to two managers with conflicting targets breaks this principle. |
| 5 | **Unity of direction** | One head and one plan for each group of activities with the same objective. | A single marketing plan, under one head, for a product launch. |
| 6 | **Subordination of individual interest to general interest** | The organisation's interest comes before any individual's or group's. | A manager does not hire a relative over a better-qualified candidate. |
| 7 | **Remuneration** | Pay should be fair and satisfy both employer and employee. | A transparent salary structure with performance-linked incentives. |
| 8 | **Centralisation** | The right degree of concentration of decision-making depends on the situation. | A chain store centralises purchasing but lets branch managers set staff rosters. |
| 9 | **Scalar chain** | A clear line of authority from top to bottom, through which communication flows, with a **gang plank** for urgent lateral contact. | See the figure above. |
| 10 | **Order** | A place for everything and everyone, and everything and everyone in its place. | Organised stores and clearly defined roles. |
| 11 | **Equity** | Kindness and justice in dealing with employees. | Equal treatment regardless of caste, religion, gender or region. |
| 12 | **Stability of tenure of personnel** | High staff turnover is costly; people need time to learn a job. | Induction and reasonable job security reduce attrition. |
| 13 | **Initiative** | Employees should be allowed to think out and carry out plans. | A suggestion scheme that rewards improvements from staff. |
| 14 | **Esprit de corps** | Union is strength: build harmony and team spirit. | Managers say "we" rather than "I", and avoid dividing staff. |

## The scalar chain and the gang plank

In Fayol's own illustration, two chains of authority run down from A: A–B–C–D–E–F on one side and A–L–M–N–O–P on the other. If F needs to deal with P, the formal route climbs all the way to A and back down, ten steps, which is slow. Fayol's answer was the **gang plank**: F and P may deal directly, provided their superiors E and O agree and are kept informed. The chain of authority is respected, and the work is not delayed.

## Fayol and Taylor compared

| Basis | Fayol | Taylor |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | Administrative management | Scientific management |
| Perspective | Top management, the whole organisation | Shop floor, the individual worker |
| Focus | General principles of management | Efficiency of tasks through work study |
| Unity of command | Strictly followed | Not followed (functional foremanship) |
| Applicability | All organisations | Mainly production and factories |
| Known as | Father of modern management theory | Father of scientific management |

## Relevance today

The principles still describe much of how organisations run: job descriptions (division of work), reporting lines (unity of command, scalar chain), HR policies (remuneration, equity, stability of tenure) and team culture (esprit de corps). Critics note that Fayol's view is top-down and assumes stable conditions; matrix structures and agile teams deliberately break unity of command. Fayol anticipated this in part: he insisted his principles were **flexible** and to be adapted to circumstances.
