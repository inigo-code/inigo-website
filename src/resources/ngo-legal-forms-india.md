An NGO can do good work informally, but to open a bank account, receive grants, give donors tax receipts and be accountable to the public, it needs a legal form. In India there are three main choices.

## 1. Public charitable trust

A trust is created when a person (the **author** or **settlor**) hands over property to **trustees** to manage for a charitable purpose, through a written **trust deed**.

- **Law:** The Indian Trusts Act, 1882 deals with private trusts. Public charitable trusts are governed by state laws where they exist (for example, the Maharashtra Public Trusts Act, 1950); elsewhere the trust deed is usually registered with the Sub-Registrar under the Registration Act, 1908.
- **Members:** a settlor and at least two trustees is common practice.
- **Management:** by the trustees named in the deed; often a small, closely held board.
- **Strengths:** simple and inexpensive to set up; stable control.
- **Limitations:** less democratic; changing trustees or objects depends on what the deed allows.

## 2. Registered society

A society is an association of people who come together for a literary, scientific or charitable purpose.

- **Law:** The Societies Registration Act, 1860, and state Acts where they apply (Tamil Nadu has the Tamil Nadu Societies Registration Act, 1975).
- **Members:** usually a minimum of seven.
- **Documents:** a memorandum of association and rules and regulations (bye-laws).
- **Management:** by an elected governing body; members vote.
- **Strengths:** democratic and suited to membership organisations.
- **Limitations:** more formalities; leadership can change through elections.

## 3. Section 8 company

A company formed to promote commerce, art, science, sports, education, research, social welfare, religion, charity or protection of the environment, which applies its profits to those objects and pays **no dividend** to members.

- **Law:** Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013; licensed by the Central Government through the Registrar of Companies.
- **Members:** at least two directors for a private company.
- **Management:** a board of directors, with company-law compliance (annual returns, audited accounts, board meetings).
- **Strengths:** the most credible form for large donors and international funders; clear governance and limited liability.
- **Limitations:** the most compliance and cost.

## Comparison at a glance

| | Trust | Society | Section 8 company |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main law | State trust law / Registration Act, 1908 | Societies Registration Act, 1860 (or state Act) | Companies Act, 2013 |
| Founding document | Trust deed | Memorandum and bye-laws | MoA and AoA |
| Minimum founders | Settlor + 2 trustees (usual) | 7 (usual) | 2 directors |
| Governance | Trustees | Elected governing body | Board of directors |
| Compliance burden | Low | Medium | High |

## Registrations that come after

A legal form is only the beginning. Most NGOs also need:

- **Income Tax registration under section 12A/12AB** of the Income Tax Act, 1961, so that the NGO's own income is exempt from tax.
- **Section 80G approval**, so that donors can claim a deduction for their donations.
- **CSR-1 registration** with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, to receive Corporate Social Responsibility funds from companies.
- **FCRA registration** under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010, before accepting any foreign contribution.
- **NGO Darpan** registration with NITI Aayog, often required for government grants.

## A worked choice

A group of college students wants to run annual sports meets for differently-abled children, mostly funded by local donors, with a small, stable core team. A **trust** suits them: it is quick and inexpensive to set up and keeps control with committed founders. If they later grow into a large membership movement, a **society** might fit better; if they seek substantial CSR or international funding, a **Section 8 company** offers the strongest governance credentials.
