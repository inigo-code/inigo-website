## Key definitions (Section 2)

- **Proposal (offer), s. 2(a):** when one person signifies to another their willingness to do or abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining that person's assent.
- **Acceptance, s. 2(b):** when the person to whom the proposal is made signifies their assent. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a **promise**.
- **Consideration, s. 2(d):** something done, abstained from or promised by the promisee or any other person, at the desire of the promisor.
- **Agreement, s. 2(e):** every promise and every set of promises forming consideration for each other.
- **Contract, s. 2(h):** an agreement enforceable by law.

Remember the formula: **Contract = Agreement + Enforceability.** All contracts are agreements, but not all agreements are contracts.

## Section 10: what makes an agreement a contract

> All agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void.

From s. 10 and the general law, the essentials are:

**1. Offer and acceptance.** There must be a lawful offer and a lawful acceptance of it. Acceptance must be absolute and unqualified (s. 7) and communicated.
*Case:* **Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt (1913)**: a person who finds a missing boy without knowing of the reward cannot claim it; there can be no acceptance without knowledge of the offer.

**2. Intention to create legal relations.** Social and domestic agreements are generally not meant to be legally binding.
*Case:* **Balfour v. Balfour (1919)**: a husband's promise of a monthly allowance to his wife was a domestic arrangement, not a contract.

**3. Lawful consideration (ss. 2(d), 23, 25).** Each party must give something in return. Consideration may be past, present or future, need not be adequate, but must be real and lawful. An agreement without consideration is void, subject to the exceptions in s. 25 (for example, a registered written promise made out of natural love and affection between near relatives).

**4. Capacity of parties (ss. 11 and 12).** A person is competent if they have reached the age of majority (18 under the Indian Majority Act, 1875), are of sound mind, and are not disqualified by law.
*Case:* **Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903)**: an agreement with a minor is **void ab initio** (void from the beginning).

**5. Free consent (ss. 13–22).** Consent is free when it is not caused by coercion (s. 15), undue influence (s. 16), fraud (s. 17), misrepresentation (s. 18) or mistake (ss. 20–22). Consent caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation makes the contract **voidable** at the option of the aggrieved party (ss. 19, 19A). A mutual mistake of fact essential to the agreement makes it **void** (s. 20).

**6. Lawful object (s. 23).** The object or consideration is unlawful if it is forbidden by law, would defeat the provisions of any law, is fraudulent, involves injury to the person or property of another, or is immoral or opposed to public policy.

**7. Not expressly declared void (ss. 24–30).** Examples include agreements in restraint of marriage (s. 26), in restraint of trade (s. 27, with exceptions), in restraint of legal proceedings (s. 28), uncertain agreements (s. 29) and wagering agreements (s. 30).

**8. Certainty and possibility of performance (ss. 29, 56).** The meaning must be certain, and an agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void.

**9. Legal formalities.** Most contracts may be oral, but where a statute requires writing, registration or stamping, those formalities must be met.
