## The idea in one line

The opposite of job dissatisfaction is not satisfaction but **no dissatisfaction**, and the opposite of job satisfaction is not dissatisfaction but **no satisfaction**. Frederick Herzberg argued that these are two separate scales, driven by two different sets of factors. That is why his theory is called the **two-factor** or **motivation-hygiene** theory.

## The study behind it

Herzberg, with Bernard Mausner and Barbara Bloch Snyderman, published the theory in *The Motivation to Work* (1959). They interviewed about 200 engineers and accountants in firms around Pittsburgh, USA, using the **critical incident method**: each person described a time they felt exceptionally good about their job and a time they felt exceptionally bad, and why.

The good stories and the bad stories turned out to be about different things. Good periods were mostly about what people *did*. Bad periods were mostly about the *conditions* around the work.

## The two sets of factors

| Hygiene factors (job context) | Motivators (job content) |
|---|---|
| Company policy and administration | Achievement |
| Supervision | Recognition for achievement |
| Relationship with supervisor, peers and subordinates | The work itself |
| Working conditions | Responsibility |
| Salary | Advancement |
| Status | Growth |
| Job security | |
| Personal life (as affected by work) | |

**Hygiene factors** got their name from medicine: like clean water and sanitation, they prevent illness but do not make anyone healthier. Poor hygiene causes dissatisfaction. Good hygiene simply avoids it.

**Motivators** are what make people put in effort and feel satisfied. Their absence leaves people neutral, not angry.

## A workplace illustration

A software company raises salaries, renovates its office and introduces a fair leave policy. Complaints drop sharply, but productivity and commitment hardly change. Herzberg would say the company has fixed **hygiene**. To motivate, it would now need to give teams ownership of whole projects, recognise good work specifically and publicly, and create real paths to grow.

The reverse also holds. A brilliant, meaningful job will still lose people if pay is unfair or the supervisor is hostile. Hygiene has to be adequate before motivators can work.

## Job enrichment: Herzberg's practical answer

In his 1968 *Harvard Business Review* article, "One more time: How do you motivate employees?", Herzberg argued that rewards and pressure (which he called "KITA") only produce short-term movement, not motivation. He recommended **job enrichment**, or **vertical loading**:

1. Remove some controls while keeping accountability.
2. Increase people's accountability for their own work.
3. Give a person a complete, natural unit of work.
4. Grant more authority and freedom in the job.
5. Make reports and feedback available directly to the worker.
6. Introduce new and more difficult tasks.
7. Assign specialised tasks that let people become experts.

Job enrichment adds depth (more control and responsibility). **Job enlargement** only adds breadth (more tasks at the same level), and Herzberg considered it of little use for motivation.

## Herzberg and Maslow compared

| | Maslow (1943) | Herzberg (1959) |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | One hierarchy of five needs | Two separate sets of factors |
| Basis | Clinical observation, theory | Interviews using critical incidents |
| Lower level | Physiological, safety, social needs | Hygiene factors |
| Higher level | Esteem, self-actualisation | Motivators |
| Pay | Satisfies lower needs | A hygiene factor |
| Key message | Needs are met in order | Removing dissatisfaction is not motivating |

## Criticisms

- **Method-bound.** People tend to credit themselves for good outcomes and blame their surroundings for bad ones. The critical incident method may have produced the two-factor split, rather than discovered it.
- **Narrow sample.** Engineers and accountants in one region of the USA in the 1950s may not represent factory workers, or employees in other cultures.
- **No overall measure of satisfaction** was used; satisfaction and productivity were assumed to move together.
- **Ambiguous factors.** Salary in particular can be both a hygiene factor and a form of recognition.

Even so, the theory remains one of the most useful in practice, because it explains a common puzzle: why pay rises and better facilities reduce complaints without raising commitment.
